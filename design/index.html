Safety Rules

Four rules that prevent almost every SMS fraud. These apply before any classifier runs — no model should be your first defence.

01

Never share an OTP — for anyone

No bank, no payment app, no delivery service, and no police officer will ever ask you to read out or forward a one-time password. An OTP authorises a transaction. Anyone who asks for it is trying to spend your money, whoever they claim to be.

02

Urgency is the tell

"Your account will be closed in 24 hours", "act now", "last warning" — real institutions give you time to verify. Fraud creates a deadline so you act before you think. Slow down. A real bank can wait.

03

Open the app yourself

Never use a link in an SMS to log into a bank. Type the address in your browser or open the installed app. A link in a message is the single easiest way to be redirected to a convincing fake login page.

04

Authority is fakeable

Caller ID and sender names can be spoofed. Police, the Cyber Cell, the CBI and delivery companies do not demand UPI payments or threaten arrest over SMS. Hang up and call the official helpline yourself.

If it looks urgent, financial, or authoritative — assume it is fraud until verified independently.

About the Detection Engine

What the classifier actually is, how it was measured, and what it cannot do. The short version: a character n-gram TF-IDF fed into a logistic regression, trained on ~19,200 labelled messages and grouped so no message template appears in both train and test.

Evaluation status: indicative — human-reviewed disagreements, one annotator

74.4% accuracy, 74.4% macro-F1 on 305 messages labelled by hand. One person labelled them. There is no second annotator, so there is no Cohen's kappa — this measures agreement with one person's judgement, not with an agreed gold standard. The annotator has reviewed the 78 disagreements; treat the score as indicative.

  • This is the hardest slice we could build, not a random sample of SMS. The messages were drawn from the review queue — the ones the corpus labeller itself refused to decide — stratified by language and source.
  • Do not compare it to the 91.9% validation score. On ordinary grouped held-out data the model scores 91.9% macro-F1. Both numbers are true; they answer different questions.
  • By language: English 75.0% (n=196), Hinglish 72.0% (n=75), Tenglish 100% (n=21), Hindi 38.5% (n=13). The Hindi and Tenglish slices are far too small to support a claim.
  • 5 messages labelled "undecidable" were excluded, counted neither right nor wrong. The annotator has reviewed the 78 initial disagreements. This review does not provide a second independent label or inter-annotator agreement; the published score is still measured against the original labels.
MODEL

What it is

Character 2–5 grams over the raw text, fed to a logistic regression that emits real probabilities directly — so the confidence you see is the model's own, not a sigmoid fitted on top of another model. Character n-grams carry the Devanagari and Telugu text, where whitespace tokenisation fails, and the romanized Hinglish and Tenglish registers. Word n-grams were removed on purpose: they latch onto "blocked", "verify" and "account" and flag genuine bank alerts, which is the exact failure this tool exists to prevent.

CLASSES

What it decides

Legitimate — expected personal, delivery or transactional message.
Marketing spam — unsolicited advertising with no attempt to take money or credentials.
Scam — pressures you to send money, reveal an OTP, or open a malicious link. When a message is both promotional and fraudulent, it is a scam.

EVALUATION

How it was measured

Every dataset was audited before use. Several claimed far more rows than they held — one "10,000 message" dataset contains 743 unique messages, and another "multilingual" set contains zero Hinglish. Splits are grouped by message template, not by row, so the model is never tested on phrasing it memorised. The final model choice was re-run across several independent grouped splits, because a difference that small is usually noise rather than a real gain.

LIMITS

What it cannot do

Tenglish and Hindi coverage is synthetic and has not been reviewed by a native speaker. A portion of the corpus is still in an unsure queue and excluded from training. The line between marketing and fraud is a judgement call, not a fact, and the model inherits every error in the labels it was given.

The published score rests on one annotator. Two rows were also found carrying contradictory labels against identical sibling messages and are reported uncorrected, because quietly fixing the gold set is how a benchmark stops meaning anything.

Signals shown on a result are not all the same thing. A model signal is the classifier's own output. A rule signal is a narrow pattern match that can be disputed. Both are shown separately so you can see which one drove the verdict.
This is a student prototype, not a security product. It can be wrong. Never rely on it to decide whether to trust a message — verify independently through an official channel, especially for banking, identity or payments. If something feels off, treat it as fraud until proven otherwise.
